Can you build a language model by hand?

The owner and the Claude measured what trained transformer weights contain, tried to write weights from those patterns without any training, and then asked how far that gets you towards law.

Owner: hordrumaBuilder: Claude Fable 5.1Repo: github.com/hordruma/Handi
In one breath

A trained model's weights are full of real, measurable structure. But that structure is the shape of the instrument, not the music. Building weights from the shape alone gives a model that is worse than random. The only authored models that work are the ones that bring in data: a word-pair table gives a model that is about as good as counting words, and a hand-built "chord" circuit, once its output is tuned by optimisation on training text with a proper held-out split, reaches half-way to a real two-word model. The trained Qwen model does know law tasks in its middle layers; a closed-form edit of its output rows raises its LegalBench score from 0.57 to 0.78. A direction for "hearsay" exists inside it, but pushing on it changes the answers without making them right.

1. The question, and the piano

A language model is a very large set of numbers, its weights. Training sets them by nudging each one millions of times. Nobody writes them by hand. This project asked whether you could.

Plain words

Think of the trained model as a piano. We can measure a lot about a finished piano: the lengths of the strings, how the frame is braced, which keys sit next to each other. The first question was whether those measurements, taken from real pianos, are regular enough to build a new piano from. The second question was whether a piano built that way plays anything. The third, once we had an instrument that made sounds, was what it takes to play a chord on purpose.

The ultimate target was law. The owner works in legal technology, and LegalBench, a public set of legal classification tasks, was the finish line throughout. Eight trained models were studied, from a one-million-parameter TinyStories model to Qwen3.5-0.8B, across three architectures.

Technical framing

Nineteen hypotheses (H1 to H19) about weight structure were measured identically on eight trained models and on families of null weight sets of the same shape. The pre-registered rule: a pattern counts as confirmed only if the trained value lies at least 5 standard deviations outside the null in at least two of three model tiers. Thirteen hypotheses were frozen before measurement; H14 to H19 were added after results were seen and are reported as amendments. The capstone, H13, asked whether weights authored from the confirmed patterns beat fresh random initialisation on held-out cross-entropy. Everything is in the repository: hypotheses in docs/hypotheses.md, dated decisions and run notes in docs/decisions.md, per-row results in results/tables/*.csv.

Cross-entropy throughout is in nats per token, lower is better. Reference lines on WikiText-103: uniform over the vocabulary 10.8, unigram (word frequencies only) 7.50, interpolated bigram (word pairs) 6.10, interpolated trigram 6.02; a trained Pythia-160M scores 3.61.

2. What is in the weights

Fourteen of nineteen structural claims held, most by enormous margins. The weights are nothing like random matrices.

Plain words

The piano is built to a pattern. A few strings carry most of the energy; the parts that read the past are shaped like "copy what was just there"; a handful of channels are much louder than the rest; the same channels are reused floor to floor; and the way words are laid out inside the model is a thin, curved sheet rather than a cloud filling the whole space. None of this was in doubt by the end. The question was whether any of it says which note each key plays.

3. Building from the blueprint

Then the test. Weights were authored from the confirmed patterns, loaded into the real model classes, and scored on held-out text. The result is the project's central negative.

Plain words

A piano built from the measurements of real pianos, with the strings attached to random keys, plays loudly and wrongly. A random pile of parts at least plays quietly. That is the whole of the first chart: every "patterned" model is far worse than the plain random one, because the patterns say how loud each part should be but not which note it should play. The one thing that worked was skipping the blueprint and wiring each key to the note that most often follows it in a book. That gives a model about as good as word counting. It knows nothing about grammar, copying or law.

Technical detail
  • Conditions. A0 fresh init; A1 every matrix given the trained singular values on random directions; A2 adds outlier dimensions and anisotropy; A3 adds the shared subspace and identity-like attention circuits; A4 an explicit previous-token plus induction circuit (copy accuracy 0.84 to 0.96 where buildable, no CE gain); A6 the H15 rotation chain; A7 golden-ratio Kronecker fill; A8 the smoothed corpus bigram table factorised in closed form (randomised SVD when untied; signed symmetric eigendecomposition when tied, sign carried in the final-norm gain) into embedding and unembedding, all residual writes and position embeddings zeroed, temperature fitted on a train slice.
  • H13 verdict: NULL. No pattern-authored condition beats fresh init. The failure is 3 to 33 nats in the wrong direction: large trained singular values on random directions produce confident wrong logits, while a fresh init is near-uniform and cannot be very wrong.
  • A8 numbers. Pythia-160M 7.27, Pythia-70M 7.39, GPT-2 7.61, TinyStories-33M 7.60, 8M 7.66, 3M 7.76, 1M 7.92, Qwen3.5-0.8B 7.66 (Qwen's own unigram line is 7.30). Copy accuracy 0 and LegalBench at majority in every A8 cell.
  • LegalBench across the ladder. 490 authored-condition comparisons; six cells beat majority at 95% confidence against about 24 expected by chance; none reproduces in a second tier.

4. Is the law still in there?

The authored models know no law. The trained Qwen3.5-0.8B scores poorly on LegalBench too, near the majority class. The owner asked whether the ability is in there and badly expressed, or simply absent. It is in there.

Plain words

Imagine a student who has read the law books but freezes at the exam and writes "No" to everything. We looked inside the model while it read each question and found that the right answer is usually visible in its middle layers, before it chooses what to say. A small, calculated correction to how the final answer is read off that state, with no training, lifts the average over ten tasks from 0.57 to 0.78, and six tasks go above 0.83. Two tasks are not decodable anywhere inside the model, so no edit can fix them.

Technical detail
  • E0 trained model, first-label-token scoring. E1 contextual calibration: each label row gets a multiple of the mean final-norm state so that the labels' mean log-probabilities on the fit fold are equal. E2 E1 plus a ridge regression from the final-norm hidden state at the answer position to the label (±1), written into the two label rows with opposite sign; ridge λ = n on standardised features. All on an untied copy of lm_head; verified by applying the real edited matrix and rescoring with the ordinary scorer (hearsay fold 0: real 0.64 vs offline 0.64).
  • Means over ten tasks: majority 0.546, E0 0.568, E1 0.673, E2 0.775, best probe 0.842.
  • The probe peaks between layers 9 and 19 on most tasks; the ability is computed mid-network and poorly expressed by the output head. This is an edit with no gradient step, consistent with the project's premise, and it adds no knowledge: it reads what is there.

5. The hearsay direction

If the model knows hearsay in its middle layers, is there a single direction in its activation space that means "this is hearsay"? Partly. There is a direction that sorts hearsay examples at 0.73, and it is specific to hearsay. But it is a label the model carries, not the lever it answers with.

Plain words

We found a dial inside the model that correlates with hearsay. Then we tried turning it. Turning it, at every layer and every position, with any strength, never made the answers more correct. The best any setting reached was the score you get by answering No to everything. What the dials did do is change the answers wholesale: a second dial, taken from the "overruling" task, turns out to be the model's plain Yes/No lever, and it can drive the fraction of Yes answers from zero to 98 percent. Turning both dials together was no better than either. The reason is simple: a dial adds the same push to every question, so it can only move the threshold, not tell one question from another.

Technical detail
  • Direction. Unit mean difference of the residual at the answer position between Yes and No hearsay items, per layer; two-fold by item parity for every reported accuracy and for the steering vectors (an item is steered by the direction fit on the other fold).
  • Specificity. Cosine with the nine other tasks' same-layer directions at most 0.21; the overruling direction reads hearsay at 0.62, the rest at 0.46 to 0.59. Logit lens of the direction is uninterpretable, as expected mid-network.
  • Causality. First run: ±4 sd at layer 11, last position, moves the fraction of Yes from 0.245 to 0.255 and changes 0 to 4% of answers on other tasks. Sweep: all layers, last and every position, γ ∈ {−4, −2, 2, 4}, directions H, O and H+O. Best accuracies: H 0.553, O 0.564, H+O 0.564. Side effects of the best H setting (layer 4, every position, +4): 2 to 30% of answers change on other tasks; best H+O (layer 14, last, −4): 0 to 35%.
  • Why a bias cannot do it. A constant added vector shifts the decision threshold; the best threshold on the model's own ranking of hearsay items is at majority (the E1 calibration edit scores 0.49). The item-specific information the probe reads is extracted by a weight (E2 0.63, probe 0.79), not a bias.
  • Form, not only law. The items projecting highest on the direction are long litigation narratives with quoted statements; the lowest are the short "On the issue of whether X, the fact that Y" template items, which are mostly No.

6. Chords: a circuit you can play

The bigram path is one key per note. The owner asked for the music theory: a circuit that says "after these two words, this third one". That is a trigram, and in a transformer it needs three parts: a way to see the previous word, a detector that fires only for a specific pair, and an output that says what comes next.

Plain words

We built a key that only sounds when two particular notes were just played. That part is exact: it fires at full strength for its own pair and essentially never otherwise. The hard part was making it play the right third note loudly enough without drowning the melody. Every closed-form way of setting that output either squashed the signal it was adding to or missed. So the owner allowed optimisation for this one part, with a strict rule: tune on training text, stop by watching separate validation text, and report once on test text never seen. That took the small model from a gain of 0.04 to a gain of 0.52, halfway to a true word-pair model, and the improvement carried over to a second corpus the fit never saw.

Technical detail
  • Layout. Residual split B + K + K with K = head_dim = 64: a bigram subspace of rank B = d − 2K (A8 recipe, untied), a fixed random ±1/√K code per token in a "current" slot, and a "previous" slot written by one RoPE previous-token head at layer 0 (copy cosine 0.99996). Embedding rows scaled to a common norm so every code slot has the same magnitude after every LayerNorm.
  • Detector. One neuron per context (a, b), the most frequent (L−1)·d_mlp pairs with n(ab) ≥ 3: input weight = code_b on the current slot + code_a on the previous slot, gain 8, bias −12. A true pair scores 15.97 ± 0.03, a half match −4, idle −12; GELU at −4 is 10⁻⁴, so idle neurons leak nothing. False-firing positions 0.1%.
  • Closed-form output (A9). Weighted least squares on the bigram subspace of r_ab = log p3(·|a,b) − log q(·|b), weights p3, ridge 10⁻², divided by gelu(4), times a global scale chosen on the validation split. LayerNorm fixes the output norm, so a correction of norm v shrinks the bigram signal by 1/√(1 + (v/r0)²); every closed-form variant (plain pseudo-inverse, replacement rows) was worse.
  • Fitted output (A10). Only the chord neurons' down-projection columns (bigram-subspace rows) are free: 3.9M weights on 70M, 21.6M on 160M. Adam, lr 3·10⁻³, batches of 8 × 512 tokens from the WikiText train split (the counted text). Early stopping on validation tokens 20k to 70k (disjoint from the 20k slice that chose the A9 scale), patience 6 evaluations, cap 4,000 steps. Test = the standard M1 slice, reported once. This is the project's first gradient step; it is confined to the chord outputs and reported on its own line.
  • Results. Pythia-70M: A9C 7.274, A9 7.235, A10 6.717 ± 0.002 (three seeds, all at the step cap); Pythia-160M: A9C 7.255, A9 7.258, A10 6.663 ± 0.006 (stopped by validation at steps 3,300 to 3,600). TinyStories CE also improves on 70M, 8.28 to 8.17, although no TinyStories text was used; on 160M 8.17 to 8.16. Copy accuracy stays 0 (no copy circuit is built). Trigram reference 6.02 is only 0.08 below the bigram line with this corpus (1.8M train tokens), so three-word contexts are mostly too rare to count; a larger corpus would raise both ceilings.

7. What this means

What it does say

The structure is real. Trained weights are far from random in a dozen measurable ways, and those ways reproduce across architectures and sizes.

The structure is the container, not the content. Everything the spectra, subspaces and outlier channels describe is invariant to which word each direction serves. Authoring the container alone produces a confident, wrong model.

Content comes from data, and exactly as much as you bring. A word-pair table gives a word-pair model. A chord circuit with a fitted output gives half of a word-pair model's remaining gap, and no more than its corpus holds.

Circuits can be written exactly. A previous-token head and a pair detector, specified on paper, work in the real architecture at the precision designed. The hard part is always the output, because it has to speak through a normalised, low-rank channel.

Trained models express less than they know. Qwen's law knowledge is linearly readable in its middle layers at 0.84 and expressed at 0.57; a calculated read-off recovers most of the difference.

What it does not say

It is not a safety bypass. Editing weights needs full weight access, and anyone with that already has cheaper known routes to any behaviour. Authoring cannot add knowledge the weights do not hold; the Rung 3 edits read out what was already there, and the chords know only their corpus.

Steering is not understanding. A direction that sorts examples is not the direction the model decides with. Pushing on it moved answers, never their correctness.

Scale is small. Eight models from 1M to 0.8B parameters; nothing here was measured on a frontier model.

Several things were decided after seeing results. H14 to H19, conditions A6 to A10, the Rung 3 edits, the hearsay work and the steering sweep are post-freeze and are marked as such in every table.
