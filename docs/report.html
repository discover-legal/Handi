Can you write a language model's weights by hand?

What Claude and I found when we tried to build a language model without training it, and what that taught us about where a trained model's knowledge lives, including its knowledge of law.

Written for the LegalQuants community. Every number on this page comes from a logged run in a git repository; the last section says how to check.

The short version

  • Trained models have real, measurable structure in their weights, and we confirmed thirteen separate kinds of it (a fourteenth, fractal structure, passed the mechanical rule but showed nothing on inspection). But that structure is about the sizes and shapes of the numbers, not about which word each number belongs to.
  • Weights written from that structure alone make a model that is worse than random. Not slightly worse: much worse, in every model we tried.
  • Nothing we wrote by hand comes close to a real trained model at predicting text.
  • On law tasks, a calculated edit to a trained model does beat the model as shipped. Qwen3.5-0.8B scores 0.57 on ten LegalBench tasks as shipped and 0.78 after we rewrote two rows of its output table using a formula fitted to labelled examples, with no fine-tuning or gradient descent. About half of that comes from removing the model's lean towards one answer (0.57 to 0.67). Two tasks could not be improved: their answers are not readable from the model's state at any layer, and the edit made them worse. On examples the edit never saw it scores 0.94 to 1.00 on three tasks.
  • But plain word counts get much of the way. A control run afterwards feeds the same formula a count of the words in each item instead of the model's internal state. With a few hundred labelled examples it scores 0.86 to 0.997 on those same three tasks, and governing-law clauses are solved by the words alone. The model's own state is worth most when labelled examples are few.
  • Across all of LegalBench the edit holds up with just 15 labelled examples per task. On the 132 tasks with two possible answers it lifts Qwen from 0.56 to 0.73 (balanced accuracy, where guessing scores 0.50), against 0.65 for word counts, and beats word counts on 105 of them. It does best on contract clauses and worst on long merger agreements.
  • It carries over to a second model. Phi-4-mini, with 3.8 billion weights, starts stronger at 0.68 on the same tasks, and the edit takes it to 0.76. On bigger Qwen models the 15-example edit stops helping: at 4 billion weights it is level with the model as shipped and below calibration alone. With more labelled examples it still adds a few points.
  • When the answer is words copied from the text, it works on one task of four. A per-word version of the read-out finds the term being defined in a sentence better than the model does itself (0.73 to 0.77 against 0.67), but not the parties to securities lawsuits.
  • Against the scores the LegalBench paper published, edited Qwen with 15 labels (0.69) lands where an 11-billion-weight model did from its prompt (0.67), and with a few hundred labels (0.79) near where GPT-4 did (0.80). That is not a like-for-like contest: the paper's models were not given the labels ours were fitted on.
  • On LexGLUE the gains are small or absent. On CaseHOLD the label-free calibration helps (0.28 to 0.46) and the read-out does not; on LEDGAR the model's state is level with word counts.
  • There is a "hearsay" pattern inside Qwen, but it is a detector, not a switch. Pushing on it changes the model's answers without making them more correct.

1. What we were trying to do

A language model is a large pile of numbers called weights. Qwen3.5-0.8B has about 800 million of them. Normally the weights are set by training: the model reads billions of words, and after every mistake every weight is nudged slightly in the direction that would have made the mistake smaller. Nobody chooses the numbers. They emerge.

We asked three questions.

  1. Trained weights are not random. Can you measure the regularities in them precisely enough to write a fresh set of weights from the regularities, and would that model do anything?
  2. If not, what can be written by hand, and how far does it get you?
  3. Take a real trained model that scores badly on law tasks. Is the legal knowledge missing, or present but badly expressed? If it is present, can a calculated edit, with no training, bring it out?

Eight trained models were studied: GPT-2 small, two Pythia models (70 million and 160 million weights), Qwen3.5-0.8B, and four small TinyStories models. Phi-4-mini and the 2, 4 and 9 billion-weight Qwen3.5 models were added later for the law work. The weight-structure experiments (section 3 and the hand-written models of section 4) were pre-registered: the hypotheses and pass rules were written down before the measurements were run. Everything on law tasks was exploratory, decided as we went and dated in the decisions file. The project was prompted by Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab's "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming" (MIT CSAIL, November 2026), in which GPT-6 Astra wrote a Go-playing network's weights as a program and edited Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights without training; we wanted to see how far hand-writing could be pushed for a language model. Prior work we build on is listed in section 10.

2. How a language model is scored

You need two scores to read this page.

Predicting the next word: nats

A language model's basic job is to guess the next word of a text. We score it on held-out text it has never seen: for each word, how much probability did the model give to the word that actually came next? The score is cross-entropy, measured in nats. Lower is better. The number itself is hard to feel, so here is the translation that makes it concrete: raising e to the power of the score gives the number of equally likely guesses the model is effectively choosing between.

Score (nats)Equivalent guesses per wordWhat a model at this level knows
10.850,000Nothing. Every word in the vocabulary is equally likely.
7.51,800How common each word is on its own ("the" is likelier than "hereinafter").
6.1450Which word tends to follow which ("of the", "United States").
6.0400Which word tends to follow a given pair of words.
3.637A trained Pythia-160M: grammar, topic, long-range context.

The test text throughout is WikiText-103, a corpus of Wikipedia articles. The 7.5, 6.1 and 6.0 lines come from simple counting models fitted to the same training text, and they are the yardsticks. One nat of improvement means the number of guesses has been cut by a factor of 2.7.

Law tasks: accuracy against the majority answer

LegalBench is a public collection of legal tasks. We started with ten with Yes/No style answers, and later ran all of them (section 5): hearsay (is this statement hearsay?), overruling (does this sentence overrule a prior case?), governing-law clauses in contracts, diversity jurisdiction, and so on. The score is accuracy: the fraction of items answered correctly. The number to beat is the majority rate: the score you would get by always giving the commoner answer. On hearsay the majority rate is 0.56, so a model scoring 0.47 is doing worse than someone who answers No to everything.

3. Can you build a model from the structure in trained weights?

No. Every model built from the measured structure alone scores far worse than a model filled with random numbers. The structure is real; it just does not say which word goes where.

First we confirmed the structure. Nineteen hypotheses about trained weights were tested against random weights of the same shape, with a strict rule: a pattern counts only if the trained value is at least five standard deviations away from random (or, for whole distributions, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance more than three times the random one) in at least two of three model sizes. Fourteen passed, most by enormous margins. For example: a few directions in each weight matrix carry most of the signal; the parts of the model that look back over earlier words are shaped like "copy what was just there"; the same handful of channels are unusually loud in every layer; and the positions of words inside the model lie on a thin curved sheet rather than filling the space.

Then we wrote fresh weights that reproduce those patterns, loaded them into the real model code, and scored them. The chart shows what happened.

Why structure alone fails. The measured patterns describe how large the numbers are and which directions they point in. A trained model has some very large numbers, and in the trained model those large numbers are attached to the right words. Reproduce the sizes without the attachments and you get a model that is loudly confident about the wrong words. A model of small random numbers is quietly unsure about everything, which scores better. Adding more of the measured structure (the loud channels, the shared directions, the copy-shaped circuits) changed nothing measurable, because none of it says which word a direction belongs to.

Method and numbers
  • Conditions: A0 fresh random initialisation; A1 every matrix given the trained singular values on random directions; A2 adds outlier dimensions and embedding anisotropy; A3 adds the cross-layer shared subspace and identity-like attention circuits; A6 adds the inter-layer rotation rule; A7 a golden-ratio (Kronecker) fill instead of Gaussian. A2, A3 and A6 are within noise of A1 and are omitted from the chart. A7 is 0.1 to 17 nats better than A1 but at its best output temperature still equals the "no idea" line.
  • The pre-registered capstone test (H13) asked whether any of A1 to A3 beats A0 on held-out cross-entropy. Verdict: null, in the wrong direction by 2 to 32 nats, in all eight models.
  • An explicitly authored copying circuit (A4) copies correctly (copy accuracy 0.84 to 0.96) but does not improve next-word prediction, since copying earlier words is a small part of predicting text.
  • Across 490 hand-built-model LegalBench comparisons, six beat the majority rate at 95% confidence, against about 24 expected by chance; none reproduced at a second model size.

4. Is anything better than the real trained model?

At predicting text, no, and it is not close. On each model's own kind of text, no hand-written model came within three nats of the trained one, which is a factor of twenty in guesses per word.

At the law tasks, yes. A formula-written edit to the trained Qwen3.5-0.8B raises its average over ten LegalBench tasks from 0.57 to 0.78, with no fine-tuning or gradient descent: a calibration and one regression, fitted in closed form on the task's own labelled examples. It works by reading out what the model's internal state already carries in a linearly readable form, though word counts carry much of it too (section 4).

How the edit works, in plain terms

By the time the model reaches "Label:", it has boiled the whole clause down to a list of 1,024 numbers. Call that list the clause's fingerprint. Every possible answer word, "Yes", "No", "cat", "the", has its own list of 1,024 numbers too. Call those the answer keys. To choose, the model lines the fingerprint up against each answer key, multiplies number by number, adds it all up, and picks whichever key gets the biggest total. That is the entire final step.

As shipped, the "Yes" and "No" keys were shaped by every Yes and No the model ever saw in training. They are tuned to things like how often No follows a colon, not whether this clause picks a law. The fingerprint carries the right information and the keys are not asking about it.

The edit makes two moves. First, level the playing field: the model leans No on average, so a flat amount is added to the Yes key until, averaged over the examples, Yes and No come out even. Second, ask the right question: take a few hundred clauses with known answers, look at their fingerprints, and find the pattern that separates the Yes ones from the No ones. That pattern is itself a list of 1,024 numbers, positive where Yes fingerprints run high, negative where No fingerprints run high, near zero where it makes no difference. Finding it is the same calculation a spreadsheet does to fit a trend line, with 1,024 columns instead of one and a brake that favours broad patterns over memorised clauses. Add the pattern to the Yes key, subtract it from the No key, and from then on the Yes key asks "does this fingerprint look like the Yes ones I was shown?" That is why it works on clauses it never saw.

In one line: the model already files each clause under a fingerprint that tells Yes from No, and we rewrote the Yes and No keys so they read that fingerprint instead of ignoring it. It is a formula because fitting the trend line is a single calculation with an exact answer: no trial and error, no repeated nudging, nothing outside those two keys changed. And the catch is in the same picture: there is one Yes key and one No key for the whole model, so whatever is written into them is asked of every question, not only this task.

Here is how the law result was obtained, step by step.

  1. As shipped, Qwen does badly. On the ten tasks its average accuracy is 0.57, barely above the majority rate of 0.55. On hearsay it scores 0.47, below the 0.56 you get by answering No every time.
  2. We looked inside. A transformer processes a prompt in layers (Qwen has 24), and at each layer there is an internal state, a list of 1,024 numbers, for the position where the answer is about to be produced. For every example in a task, we recorded that state at every layer.
  3. The answer is readable in there. A linear probe is the simplest possible test: can a weighted sum of those 1,024 numbers, with weights fitted on half the examples, predict the correct answer on the other half? For seven of the ten tasks it can, at 0.84 to 1.00, usually from the middle layers (the best layer is picked on the same items, so these are slight overestimates). For hearsay the probe reaches 0.79 at layer 17. So the answer is linearly readable from the model's state, though the model does not say it. Readable is not the same as understood: the word-count control below shows how much of it the words themselves carry.
  4. We wrote the read-out into the model. The last step of a transformer is a table with one row per word; the answer is whichever row scores highest against the final internal state. We changed two rows, the ones for "Yes" and "No". First a correction that removes the model's built-in lean towards one answer (it says No too often). Then the probe's weights, added to the Yes row and subtracted from the No row. Both are closed-form calculations; the model is never trained.
  5. We tested it honestly. Each task's examples were split in two. The edit was calculated from one half and scored on the other, then the halves were swapped. Every reported number is an example scored by an edit that never saw it. We also applied the edited rows to the real model file and re-ran the ordinary scorer to check the offline calculation against the live model: on hearsay the live model scored 0.638 and the offline calculation 0.617, one example in 47 apart (the live model runs in bfloat16, the offline calculation in float64).

What this does and does not show. The edit adds no legal knowledge. It is a calculated classifier written into the model's own output table, so that the plain model, with no wrapper and no prompt tricks, answers better. Anyone who trains a small classifier on top of a model's internal states is doing something similar; the difference here is that the result lives inside the weights and was computed by formula. It also shows something about small models in general: they can know more than they say, and the gap is sometimes recoverable without training.

Method and numbers
  • E0: the trained model, first-label-token scoring. E1: label-bias calibration in the spirit of contextual calibration (Zhao et al. 2021) and batch calibration (Zhou et al. 2024), estimated from the fit items: each label row shifted along the mean final-state direction so the labels' mean log-probabilities on the fit half are equal. E2: E1 plus a ridge regression (λ = n on standardised features) from the final-norm state at the answer position to the label, written into the two label rows with opposite signs. Edits on an untied copy of the output matrix.
  • Means over ten tasks: majority 0.546, E0 0.568, E1 0.673, E2 0.775, best probe 0.842. Per-task rows and the per-layer probe curve are in results/tables/rung3.csv.
  • Verification on hearsay, fold 0: edited matrix in the live model with the standard scorer 0.638; offline calculation 0.617 (one example of 47).

Does it hold on examples it never saw?

The half-and-half test already scores every example with an edit that did not see it. But three tasks have more examples in LegalBench than the 500 we sampled, so we could go further: fit the edit on the whole sample, then score examples that played no part in anything.

Those are the same numbers the half-and-half test gave, within a point or two, so nothing was being memorised. One caution and one test on the two near-perfect tasks. The caution: both look lexically easy, since a governing-law clause usually says "governed by the laws of" and a defining sentence says "means" or "is defined as", so a classifier that merely spots the phrase would score well. The test: we deleted those phrases from the never-seen items (so "construed in accordance with the laws of the State of California" became "[...] the State of California"), and separately paraphrased them into plain words ("read under the rules of"). The edited model still scored 0.997 on governing law and 0.97 on definitions either way, while the model as shipped fell to coin-flip level. So the edit is not just spotting the stock phrase, though only 60% of the definition Yes items carry one of the listed phrases, so that test covers part of the task. That is not the same as showing the model understands the clause, as the control below shows. And LegalBench holds no more examples of either: the 876 governing-law clauses are the whole task (they come from the CUAD contract dataset), and the 1,337 definition sentences are the whole task. Every one of them has now been scored by an edit that did not see it.

The edit is also strictly one task at a time. It rewrites the " Yes" and " No" rows, which every Yes/No prompt reads, so one task's edit changes between 2% and 97% of another task's answers, and summing all ten edits into one table leaves only three tasks working. A model edited for several tasks would need its own label pair per task, or an edit deeper than the output table.

A control: how far do plain word counts get?

The edit learns from labelled examples, so the fair question is how much of the score comes from that learning rather than from the model. We kept the formula exactly as it is and fed it, instead of the model's 1,024-number fingerprint, a count of each word in the item (words that appear in at least two of the labelled examples). We also ran it on a model with random, untrained weights, and with the labels shuffled. Every number is accuracy on the never-seen examples, with the edit fitted on 500 labelled ones.

Three things follow. Shuffled labels give almost nothing, so the score is not an artefact of fitting 500 examples. The trained model is worth something over the words: 3 points on overruling, 12 on definitions. And governing law is solved by word counts (0.997), so its perfect score says nothing about what the model knows, and our earlier reading that the edit "reads what the clause does" is withdrawn for that task. Even a model with random weights reaches most of the score, because with a few hundred labels the formula does most of the work itself. If the trained model pays off, it should be when labels are few. That is what the next section tests.

5. Does it work across all of LegalBench?

Yes, with only 15 labelled examples per task. On the 132 LegalBench tasks with two possible answers, the edit takes Qwen from 0.56 to 0.73, ahead of word counts at 0.65. It also works with more than two answers, with whole prompts, and on a second model. When the answer is words copied from the text, it works on one task of four.

LegalBench (Guha et al. 2023) has 162 tasks, and 155 of them have a fixed set of answers. We ran 153 (the other two do not fit the row edit; see below). To make the test harder and closer to real use, each edit was fitted on just 15 labelled examples drawn at random and scored on every other item in the task. This was repeated with 20 different draws and averaged. The word-count control runs beside it, fitted on the same 15 examples.

Many LegalBench tasks are lopsided: in some, nine items in ten have the same answer, and plain accuracy rewards always giving that answer. So from here on we use balanced accuracy: the share answered correctly is worked out separately for each possible answer, then averaged. Guessing, or always giving the same answer, scores 0.50 on a two-answer task.

Across the 132 tasks, the edit beats the model as shipped on 124 and word counts on 105. It reaches 0.90 or more on 22 tasks; word counts do that on one. Given more labels, word counts close part of the gap: when the edit is fitted on half of each task's items (up to 250 labels), it scores 0.82 against 0.77 for word counts. The model's own state is worth 8 points with 15 labels and 5 with a few hundred, which is what you would hope for: it matters most when labels are scarce.

More than two answers

21 tasks have three to eight possible answers: for example the five classes of trademark distinctiveness in abercrombie, or which way a Canadian tax appeal went. The formula extends without change of idea: one read-out per answer, each written into that answer's row. With 15 examples spread over up to eight answers, some answers get one example or none, so we also tried 30, 60 and 120.

Reading the whole prompt

Every run up to this point cut prompts longer than 1,024 tokens to their last 1,024. On 90 tasks that sometimes left the model reading the item without the instruction or the worked examples at the top of the prompt. We reran those tasks with the whole prompt. With 15 examples, the edit rises from 0.64 to 0.67 on average, better on 76 tasks and worse on 12. Word counts stay at 0.59, because they always read the whole item. The edit now beats word counts on 69 of the 89 tasks instead of 59. The biggest gain is on Learned Hands, people describing legal problems in their own words: 0.69 to 0.79 over 15 tasks. One task, learned_hands_housing, has a 6,593-token prompt that does not fit whole on our 8 GB graphics card, so it was not rerun.

A second model

To see whether this is something about Qwen, we ran the same prompts and the same formula on Phi-4-mini-instruct, a 3.8-billion-weight model from Microsoft with an MIT licence. Phi reads prompts up to 4,096 tokens on our card (17 tasks had longer ones, cut to their last 4,096).

Read it with care. The paper's models answered from a written prompt with a handful of worked examples; ours were fitted on the task's own labelled examples, which is a different and easier setting, and the items scored differ. The fair reading is narrower than "a small model beats GPT-4": with 15 labels, a 0.8-billion-weight model plus a formula lands where an 11-billion-weight model did from its prompt, and with a few hundred labels it lands near where GPT-4 did. The paper's models were not given the same labels, and a large model given them would most likely score higher too. On the four extraction tasks the paper's GPT-4 is ahead of our read-out on all four (definitions 0.82 against 0.77; plaintiffs 0.92 against 0.75; company defendants 0.65 against 0.12; individual defendants 0.30 against 0.22).

Answers copied from the text

Four LegalBench tasks ask for words from the text rather than a label: the term a sentence defines (definition_extraction), and the plaintiffs, company defendants and individual defendants named in securities class-action complaints (the three ssla tasks). The output-row edit cannot do these, because there is no fixed answer row to rewrite. But the same calculation can be pointed at each word of the text instead: from the model's internal state at that word, is this word part of the answer? Words that score above zero are copied out. This read-out sits beside the model rather than inside it: the model's own written reply is unchanged, and the answer comes from reading the model's state at every word.

Scores use LegalBench's own scoring code. The read-out works on definitions: from 30 examples it beats the model's own answer, reaching 0.77 when fitted on half the items, far above word counts. The other three do not work, each for a visible reason. Company defendants must be given by their full legal name ("Akorn, Inc."), but the complaint mostly says "Akorn", so copying words from the text can reach only 0.11, while the model, which writes the full name, gets 0.34. On individual defendants the read-out ties the model; the best possible score is only 0.31 because LegalBench's scorer adds a penalty once for every name, which punishes complaints with many defendants. And on plaintiffs, 378 of 500 complaints name none: the read-out's 0.75 is simply answering "Not named" every time, which scores the same. A version weighted to pick out the rare name words picks too many and gets worse with more examples. No version found the named plaintiffs.

Two more benchmarks: LexGLUE

LexGLUE is the other standard benchmark for legal language. It has no prompts, so each task needed a decision. Two were run on Qwen.

CaseHOLD gives a passage citing a case and five candidate holdings, and asks which one the cited case stands for. Our prompt lists the passage, the five holdings numbered 0 to 4, and "Answer:". Guessing scores 0.20 and the model as shipped scores 0.28. Removing its lean towards certain numbers, which uses no labels at all, takes it to 0.46. The read-out on top then makes it worse, 0.35 with 15 labels and 0.42 with 120, and word counts stay at chance. That makes sense: the right number depends on which of five holdings matches the passage, and its position is random, so there is no fixed pattern for "the answer is 3" to learn.

LEDGAR asks which of 100 types a contract provision is ("Governing Laws", "Terminations" and so on). The output-row edit does not fit 100 answers whose names share first tokens, so here the read-out sits beside the model, as for the extraction tasks: one read-out per type on the model's internal state, fitted on 1,000 labelled provisions, scored on all 10,000 test provisions. It gets 0.685 of them right, against 0.660 for word counts; averaged over types (macro-F1) it is slightly behind word counts, 0.48 against 0.49. With 1,000 labels for 100 types, the model's state adds about as much as the words do.

What was not run

  • Three tasks with open answers need knowledge or arithmetic, not reading: rule_qa (free-text answers), citation_prediction_open (naming a case) and sara_numeric (a tax amount in dollars). Nothing here adds knowledge, so there is no equivalent.
  • Two tasks with fixed answers do not fit the row edit: successor_liability, whose answers are sets of several labels, and unfair_tos, where two labels start with the same token.
  • learned_hands_housing with its whole prompt, which does not fit on the graphics card, and a Nemotron model, which needs its own model code.
  • LexGLUE SCOTUS, whose Supreme Court opinions (12,500 tokens typical, up to 91,600) do not fit the graphics card whole, and LexGLUE's three tasks with several answers per item.
Method and numbers
  • Prompts: each task's LegalBench base_prompt.txt. Sample: all items, or 500 drawn with seed 0. The 15-example protocol: 15 items drawn at random (not balanced by label) from the collected items, 20 draws, seed 300; the edit and the word-count ridge are fitted on the same 15 and scored on every other collected item; λ = 15. "Half the items": two-fold by item parity.
  • Means over the 132 two-answer tasks, 15 examples, balanced accuracy: as shipped 0.562, calibrated 0.659, edited 0.733, word counts 0.651; plain accuracy 0.590, 0.657, 0.788, 0.711 (majority 0.595). Two-fold: edited 0.816, word counts 0.765.
  • More than two answers: one standardised ridge per answer (to +1 for that answer's items, −1 for the others), U[t_k] += α w_k + (α b_k / p̄) μ̂, α = 2 × spread of the calibrated gap / spread of the read-out, both as root mean square over answer pairs. For two answers this is exactly the two-row edit (rows agree to 3e-16). On the 11 tasks run at every size: edited 0.414, 0.472, 0.526, 0.617 at 15, 30, 60, 120; word counts 0.345, 0.397, 0.469, 0.584; as shipped 0.309.
  • Whole prompts: 89 tasks, 15 examples: edited 0.636 cut, 0.670 whole; as shipped 0.483, 0.503. At 15, 30, 60, 120 examples whole: edited 0.670, 0.703, 0.744 (80 tasks), 0.790 (55 tasks); word counts 0.589, 0.637, 0.687, 0.733.
  • Phi-4-mini-instruct: layers on the GPU, its token table on the CPU, label scores computed in float32; same formula. Two-fold, two-answer tasks: edited 0.842 (Qwen 0.816).
  • Extraction read-out: the final state at the last token of every word of the item's text; a word is +1 inside an occurrence of the answer (matched whole and by comma-separated pieces, ignoring case and punctuation). One standardised ridge, λ = number of fit words, threshold 0; the term task returns the single best-scoring run of words, the ssla tasks every run. Reported both weighted (answer words and other words counting equally) and unweighted; the table shows weighted for the first three tasks and unweighted for plaintiffs. Word baseline: the word, its neighbours and a capital-letter flag.

6. Is there a "hearsay" switch inside the model?

There is a hearsay detector, not a switch. One direction in the model's internal state separates hearsay examples from non-hearsay at 0.73 and is specific to hearsay. But pushing the model along that direction never makes its answers more correct. It only changes them.

Since the probe found the answer inside the model, we asked a sharper question: is there a single direction that means "hearsay"? Take the internal state at each layer, average it over the Yes examples and over the No examples, and subtract. That difference is a direction. Fitted on half the examples, it sorts the other half at 0.73 at the best layers. It is not the same as the model's general Yes/No direction (the two are nearly perpendicular), and the corresponding directions for the other nine tasks do not sort hearsay (0.46 to 0.62).

Then we tested whether it is a switch. Steering means adding a multiple of the direction to the internal state while the model runs, and seeing what it answers. We tried every layer, adding at the answer position only or at every position, at four strengths, and we also tried the "overruling" task's direction, which reads hearsay at 0.62, and the two added together.

Why a direction cannot be a switch here. Steering adds the same push to every example. That can only move the model's threshold between Yes and No; it cannot tell one example from another. The best threshold on the model's own ranking of hearsay examples is no better than majority. What the probe and the two-row edit do differently is multiply each example's state by a set of weights, which produces a different push for each example. That is the difference between a bias and a classifier, and it is why the edit works and steering does not.

One more caution. The examples that score highest on the hearsay direction are long litigation narratives with a quoted statement in them; the lowest are short template items of the form "On the issue of whether X, the fact that Y", which are mostly No. The direction tracks the form of a hearsay problem at least as much as the law of hearsay.

Method and numbers
  • Direction: unit mean difference of the residual state at the answer position between Yes and No items, per layer, two-fold by item parity for every reported number; steering vectors likewise use the direction fitted on the other half, scaled by that half's projection standard deviation.
  • Specificity: cosine with the output Yes-minus-No vector −0.02; cosine with the other nine tasks' same-layer directions at most 0.21.
  • Sweep: 24 layers × {last position, every position} × strength ∈ {−4, −2, +2, +4} standard deviations × {hearsay, overruling, both}. Best accuracies: hearsay 0.553, overruling 0.564, both 0.564; unsteered 0.468. Side effects on the other nine tasks at the best settings: 0 to 35% of answers change.
  • The calibration-only edit (E1, a pure threshold shift) scores 0.49 on hearsay. The best of the 576 steering settings reached 0.564, the majority rate, and that best setting was picked on the same items it was scored on.

7. What we conclude

  • The structure in trained weights is real and large, and it reproduces across three architectures and three sizes. It describes the container: how big the signals are and which directions they use.
  • The container is not the content. The content is the pairing of each direction with a word, and none of the structural measurements see it. Writing the container without the content gives a confidently wrong model.
  • A trained model's state can carry more than it says, but less than the first results suggested. This echoes earlier findings (Burns et al. 2023; Orgad et al. 2025, "LLMs Know More Than They Show") and Takehi et al.'s finding, from their Gemma 3 270M edits, that weight edits can uncover an ability but not add one. The answers are linearly readable inside Qwen at 0.84 on average on the first ten tasks and expressed at 0.57, and a formula recovers most of that difference. But the same formula on plain word counts recovers much of it too. The model's own state adds most when labels are few (8 points of balanced accuracy at 15 labels, 5 at a few hundred) and on contract clauses; on long merger agreements it adds nothing.
  • It is not one model's quirk, but it fades with size. The same formula works across 153 LegalBench tasks, with more than two answers, and on a second, stronger model. On the 98 two-answer tasks that Qwen3.5 0.8B, 2B and 4B all finished, the 15-example edit adds 0.19, 0.09 and 0.01 (0.58 to 0.77, 0.72 to 0.81, 0.81 to 0.82), and at 4B calibration alone (0.83) beats it. Fitted on half of each task's items it still adds 0.26, 0.16 and 0.07. A 9-billion-weight run (4-bit) was still in progress when this was written; on the 74 two-answer tasks it had finished (the alphabetically first, including all 38 contract-clause CUAD tasks), the 15-example edit is level with the model as shipped (0.87 to 0.86), calibration alone does slightly better (0.87), and the half-items edit adds 0.05.

What we do not claim

  • No new capability was created. Nothing here adds knowledge to a model. The law edit reads out what the model's state already carries, in line with Takehi et al.'s finding that weight edits can uncover an ability but not add one.
  • This is not a safety bypass. Every edit here requires full access to the weights, and anyone with that access already has cheaper, well-known ways to change a model's behaviour. A direction that sorts examples is not the direction the model decides with, and pushing on it did not make the model do anything it could not already do.
  • The models are small. One million to 3.8 billion weights. Nothing was measured on a frontier model.
  • Some of this was decided after seeing results. Six of the nineteen hypotheses, the law edits, the steering work, the controls, the LegalBench-wide runs, the second model and the extraction read-out were added after the pre-registered results were in. They are exploratory, and dated in the decisions file. No fine-tuning or gradient descent was used anywhere in the law work; the read-outs are closed-form fits on labelled examples, which is a form of supervised learning.

8. How to pull a classifier out of a model yourself

The whole of section 4 is about forty lines of Python once the model is loaded. Here is the recipe, written so you can do it to a model of your own. A fuller version with the code is in the repository: docs/cookbook.md.

  1. Pick a task with two one-token answers (" Yes" / " No" work in most tokenisers; check with the tokeniser) and a prompt template that ends exactly where the answer goes, such as Label:. Gather labelled examples: 15 already help, a few hundred are better. More than two answers works the same way, with one read-out per answer (section 5).
  2. Record the internal state. Run each prompt through the model and keep two things from the last position: the final internal state h (the vector after the final normalisation, 1,024 numbers in Qwen3.5-0.8B) and the full output scores. In Hugging Face this is hidden_states[-1] at the last position and lm_head applied to it.
  3. Split the examples in two halves. Everything that follows is fitted on one half and scored on the other, then swapped. Never report the in-sample number; in-sample every task scored 0.89 to 1.00 and that means nothing.
  4. Check the answer is readable. Fit a ridge regression from h to the label (+1 / −1) on the fit half, with the regularisation set to the number of examples on standardised features. Score the other half. If it is at the majority rate, stop: the answer is not readable here and no edit of the output can help.
  5. Remove the model's lean. Compute each label's average log-probability on the fit half. Shift each label's output row along the direction of the average state by the amount that makes those averages equal. This alone took the ten-task mean from 0.57 to 0.67.
  6. Write the read-out into the rows. Scale the ridge weights so their spread across examples is twice the spread of the (calibrated) Yes-minus-No score, add them to the Yes row and subtract them from the No row, with the intercept carried along the average-state direction. If the model shares one table for input and output, untie it first or the edit also changes how the words are read in.
  7. Verify on the live model. Put the edited rows into the real weights and re-run your ordinary scorer on the held-out half. It should match the offline arithmetic closely; ours was one example in 47 apart (live model in bfloat16, offline arithmetic in float64).
  8. Run the word-count control. Fit the same formula on counts of the words in each example instead of h, on the same labels. If it scores as well, the model is adding nothing you could not get from the words.
  9. Know what you have built. One task per label pair: the edit changes the model's Yes/No behaviour for every other prompt that uses those tokens. It adds no knowledge. And the read-out at the final layer is usually a few points below the best middle layer; reaching that would need an edit deeper in the network, which this recipe does not do.

9. Glossary

Weights
The numbers inside a model. Training sets them; this project wrote them.
Layer
One stage of a transformer. Qwen3.5-0.8B has 24; Pythia-70M has 6. Each layer reads the internal state and adds to it.
Internal state (residual stream)
The list of numbers (512 to 1,024 of them) that a transformer carries for each word position from layer to layer. Everything the model "knows" about the text at that point is in it.
Cross-entropy, nats
The next-word prediction score. e to the power of the score is the equivalent number of equally likely guesses. Lower is better.
Accuracy, majority rate
Fraction of items answered correctly; and the score from always giving the commoner answer.
Balanced accuracy
Accuracy worked out separately for each possible answer, then averaged. Guessing scores 1 divided by the number of answers, however lopsided the task.
Word-count control
The same formula fitted on counts of the words in each item instead of the model's internal state. It shows how much of a score the words alone can earn.
F1
For answers that are lists of names: a score that balances finding every right name against adding wrong ones. 1 is perfect.
Linear probe
A weighted sum of the internal state, fitted to predict a label. The simplest test of whether information is present in a readable form.
Direction
A fixed pattern of numbers in the internal state; here, the average difference between Yes and No examples.
Steering
Adding a multiple of a direction to the internal state while the model runs.
Seed
The random starting point of a run. Repeating with several seeds shows how much a result depends on luck. The weight-structure charts average three or five; the LegalBench charts average 20 random draws of labelled examples.
